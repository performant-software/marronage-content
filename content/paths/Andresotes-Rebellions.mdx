      Cacao production increased substantially from 1607 onward and became Venezuela’s main staple crop throughout the eighteenth century, which sustained commercial networks with New Spain, Curaçao, and the Canary Islands. 

      Both the Yaracuy Valleys (under the jurisdiction of Nirgua) in the northwest – covering areas like Valencia, San Felipe, Barquisimeto, and Coro  – and the Tuy Valleys on the north-central coast  – in proximity to Caracas and La Guaira  –  became important areas of cacao production. On average, cacao comprised 78.5% of all registered products leaving the province by 1650, with a slight decrease to this initial boom between 1650 and 1660 due to a blight epidemic that affected many crops along with commercial competitions with Guayaquil producers who also entered the New Spain market.  

      By the 1670s, cacao production and trade continued at growing rates, and mobilized through New Spain and Curaçao, which became major export centers, allowing revenues to go directly to local producers. Curaçao ports and Dutch traders in particular became central in this commercial exchange. Seized by the Dutch in 1634, the island of Curaçao allowed merchants to travel along the coast of Venezuela, selling goods like textiles, olive oil, alcohol and enslaved people in exchange for cacao, plugin into a critical trade corridor that linked Circum-Caribbean ports to major cities and cacao-producing regions.

      The rise of cacao commerce beyond Spanish control became a problem that required a solution after the Bourbons assumed the Spanish throne in the early eighteenth century. 

      Centralizing policies introduced under this dynasty aimed to restrict and police the centuries-long tradition of unsanctioned trade and commercial networks between the Province of Venezuela, Curaçao, and other parts of the Caribbean. 

      Among the resolutions, the Spanish crown delegated economic control of the province to the Compañía Guipuzcoana, a Basque chartered company, by a Royal Decree dated May 25, 1728, to designate new brokers who could control Venezuela’s trade on behalf of the Spanish crown.

      The Caracas Company established warehouses in La Guaira and Puerto Cabello and employed soldiers to defend the coasts against foreign invasions and seize contraband goods. They also sought to enforce the monopoly trade between Spain and Venezuela, imposing taxes and regulations on commerce as cacao continued to expand well into the late eighteenth century.
---

Andresote’s uprising, which lasted from 1728 until 1732 when he fled to Curaçao, became the most significant opposition to the Compañía Guipuzcoana in its early years in the Province of Venezuela. 

Colonial officials documented this episode in a considerable number of field reports, interrogations, witnesses’ accounts, letters, autos, memoriales, and Cabildo minutes, a long trail of paperwork that offers a detailed view into the geographic extent, economic drive, and social composition of this movement. 

This information reveals the long-standing alliances that sustained northwestern Venezuela’s economic and spatial orders before the Compañía Guipuzcoana’s arrival, and shows the conflict among colonial civil officials as they responded to a problem they could neither suppress militarily nor resolve administratively. 

The uprising was not just an anti-regime movement, but a long, spatial conflict centered on cimarrón geographies that made northwestern Venezuela's cocoa economy possible while operating beyond and against colonial administrative control.
